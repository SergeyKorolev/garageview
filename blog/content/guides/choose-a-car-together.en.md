## Write Individual Needs Before Sharing Favourites

When embarking on the journey of choosing a car together, it is crucial to start by writing down individual needs before diving into personal favorites. Each regular user, driver, and passenger should have a voice in this process. This ensures that the car chosen will meet the practical requirements of everyone involved.

Begin by listing the specific needs of each person who will regularly use the car. This could include factors such as the number of seats required, storage space, fuel efficiency, or any special features like child safety locks or easy accessibility for elderly passengers. For example, if one user frequently transports sports equipment, ample trunk space might be a necessity.

It is important to note that the person paying for the car does not automatically have veto power over access essentials. While financial contributions are significant, they should not overshadow the practical needs of other users. Documenting these needs helps in ensuring that all voices are heard and considered equally.

## Separate Essentials from Preferences and Open Questions

Once individual needs are documented, the next step is to separate essentials from preferences and open questions. Essentials are non-negotiable requirements that must be met for the car to be functional for its intended use. These might include aspects like the number of doors, fuel type, or specific safety features. Physical fit, such as seat comfort or ease of entry and exit, cannot be averaged away and must be considered for each user.

Preferences, on the other hand, are features that would be nice to have but are not critical. These might include the color of the car, brand preferences, or additional tech features like a sunroof or premium sound system. It's important to recognize that disagreements in taste should not be confused with factual needs. For instance, while one person might prefer a leather interior, it should not outweigh the essential need for a car with good fuel efficiency.

Open questions are areas where more information is needed before making a decision. These could involve uncertainties about the car's performance, maintenance costs, or resale value. Documenting these questions will help guide further research and discussions.

## Agree on a Tie-Breaker Before Viewings

Before setting out to view potential cars, it is essential to agree on a tie-breaker strategy. This strategy should prioritize essentials first, ensuring that all must-have criteria are met before considering other factors. If there is unresolved evidence or conflicting information about a particular car, it should be the next point of investigation. This might involve seeking additional reviews, test-driving the car, or consulting with a mechanic.

Preference priorities should be jointly agreed upon, rather than decided by a majority override. This means that if there is a split opinion on a preference, it should not automatically be dismissed or accepted based on the number of people in favor. Instead, a discussion should take place to understand why that preference is important to each person and how it aligns with the overall goals for the car.

Documenting who actually uses the car and which routine makes a need important is crucial. For example, if one person uses the car daily for commuting, their need for fuel efficiency might take precedence over another's preference for a specific brand. By clearly outlining these roles and routines, the decision-making process becomes more transparent and focused on meeting the practical needs of all users involved.

In summary, choosing a car together requires careful consideration of individual needs, a clear distinction between essentials and preferences, and a pre-agreed tie-breaker strategy. By documenting these elements, the group can make a well-informed decision that satisfies the practical requirements and preferences of all involved.

## Fictional Shared Shortlist

Alex and Sam have narrowed down their car choices to three potential options, each with its own set of pros and cons. The primary requirements are that the car must have an automatic transmission for Alex, comfortable access for Sam, and enough space to fit a pushchair. Here's a summary of their shortlist:

| Car | Transmission | Access | Pushchair Fit | Next Action |
|-----|--------------|--------|---------------|-------------|
| A   | Automatic (claimed) | Access not tried | Fit unknown | Test drive and check access/fit |
| B   | Manual (confirmed) | Not assessed | Fit unknown | Exclude: transmission fails |
| C   | Automatic (confirmed) | Access acceptable to Sam after visit | Fit still untested | Test pushchair fit |

### Car A
- **Transmission**: The seller claims Car A has an automatic transmission, which meets Alex's requirement. However, this needs to be verified during a test drive.
- **Access**: Sam has not yet tried accessing Car A, so this remains an unknown factor. It's crucial to test this in person to ensure comfort.
- **Pushchair Fit**: The capacity to fit a pushchair is still unknown, necessitating a physical test with the pushchair.
- **Next Action**: Arrange a test drive to confirm the automatic transmission and evaluate both access and pushchair fit.

### Car B
- **Transmission**: Car B has been confirmed to have a manual transmission, which disqualifies it for Alex.
- **Access**: Access has not been assessed. There is no need to invent another failure to justify excluding a car that already misses an essential.
- **Pushchair Fit**: The fit remains unknown; the confirmed manual transmission is enough to stop considering this candidate under the current brief.

### Car C
- **Transmission**: The automatic transmission has been confirmed, satisfying Alex's requirement.
- **Access**: Sam found the access acceptable after a visit, making this a viable option.
- **Pushchair Fit**: The fit for the pushchair has not yet been tested, which is the next step.
- **Next Action**: Test the pushchair fit to ensure it meets their needs.

## Viewing Tasks

When viewing the cars, Alex and Sam have specific tasks to ensure all their requirements are met:

- **Alex Checks Driving Setup**: Alex should focus on verifying the automatic transmission and assessing the driving setup. This includes checking the ease of use of the controls and overall driving comfort.

- **Sam Checks Access**: Sam needs to evaluate the ease of getting in and out of the car. This involves testing door width, seat height, and any other factors that contribute to comfortable access.

- **Both Load Pushchair with Seller Permission**: It's essential to test whether the pushchair fits in the car's boot. With the seller's permission, Alex and Sam should attempt to load and unload the pushchair to ensure it fits comfortably.

- **Each Write Notes Before Persuasion**: After the viewing, both Alex and Sam should independently write down their observations and thoughts. This helps in making an informed decision without being swayed by each other's immediate reactions.

## After Both Pass All Essentials

Once both cars A and C pass the essential requirements, Alex and Sam can focus on additional preferences, such as comfort versus parking feel. This decision involves subjective criteria that may vary in importance to each person.

- **Sample Manual Ranking**: Instead of relying on a numerical score, Alex and Sam should rank their preferences manually. For example, they might prioritize interior comfort over ease of parking or vice versa.

- **Document Reasons and Differing Weights**: It's important to document the reasons behind each preference and how much weight each factor holds. Alex might prioritize a smooth driving experience, while Sam might value easy parking more.

By clearly outlining their preferences and documenting their reasons, Alex and Sam can make a well-informed decision that balances both their needs and desires.

## A 20-Minute Shortlist Meeting

When choosing a car together, a structured 20-minute meeting can help streamline the decision-making process. Here's a breakdown of how to effectively use this time:

### 3 Minutes: Restate the Brief

Begin by restating the agreed-upon criteria for the car. This ensures everyone is aligned and focused on the same goals. For example, you might say, "We are looking for a family car with ample boot space, good fuel efficiency, and within our budget of €20,000." Clarifying these points helps prevent discussions from veering off track.

### 5 Minutes: Evidence Updates

Each participant should present any new evidence or findings since the last discussion. This could include new listings, reviews, or test drive experiences. For instance, "I found a listing for a [Fictional Model] that fits our budget and has excellent reviews for safety." Make sure to note the source of this information for future reference.

### 5 Minutes: Discuss Disagreements

Address any disagreements openly. Focus on understanding different perspectives rather than winning an argument. For example, if someone prefers a different model, ask them to explain their reasoning. This could be due to personal experiences or specific needs that weren't initially considered.

### 5 Minutes: Assign Checks

Assign tasks to resolve any outstanding questions or disagreements. This might include scheduling a test drive, checking insurance costs, or verifying seller claims. Clearly assign who will handle each task and set a deadline. For example, "Alex will check the insurance rates for the [Fictional Model] by Friday."

### 2 Minutes: Record the Decision

Conclude the meeting by summarizing any decisions made and the next steps. Record these in a shared document or tool for easy access. For instance, "We have decided to focus on the [Fictional Model] and will reassess after Alex checks the insurance rates."

## Conversation Templates

Effective communication is key when choosing a car together. Here are some templates to guide your discussions:

- **Preference Statement**: "I prefer [Car A] because it has more boot space, which is important for our family trips."
- **Evidence Presentation**: "The evidence I have is from a recent review on [Website], which highlights its reliability."
- **Question to Change Mind**: "What information could change my mind about [Car A]?"

### Disagreement Script

When disagreements arise, use a script that focuses on personal needs without assigning blame:

- "I understand you prefer [Car B], but my personal need is for more boot space due to our family size. Can we explore options that meet both our needs?"

## Dissent Log

Keeping a dissent log helps track disagreements and their resolutions. Here's a fictional example involving a pushchair:

- **Reason/Evidence or Taste**: "I believe the [Fictional Model] is too small for our pushchair."
- **What Action Resolves**: "Measure the boot space and compare it with the pushchair dimensions."
- **Who/When**: "Jamie will measure the boot space by Thursday."

## New vs Used Timing Disagreement

When a group is divided between ordering a new car with custom features and meeting a specific deadline with a used car, it's crucial to gather actual quotes and assess temporary transport options. Begin by contacting dealers for quotes on both new and used vehicles. For new cars, inquire about the lead time for custom orders, as this can vary significantly. For used cars, ask for detailed condition and history reports to ensure transparency.

Consider the feasibility of temporary transport solutions if the new car's delivery timeline doesn't align with your deadline. Options might include short-term rentals or car-sharing services. Document the quotes, delivery timelines, and temporary transport costs to facilitate a well-informed decision.

## Reusable Consensus Worksheet

Creating a consensus worksheet can streamline the decision-making process. This worksheet should include:

- **Requirements**: List each person's must-have features and preferences. This could include fuel efficiency, seating capacity, or specific technology.
- **Evidence**: Gather supporting documents such as quotes, condition reports, and transport options.
- **Individual View**: Capture each person's perspective on the options, noting any strong preferences or deal-breakers.
- **Shared Unknown**: Identify any information gaps that need addressing, such as unclear maintenance history or unverified seller claims.
- **Decision Rule**: Agree how to compare preferences after all users’ essentials are met. An average score or majority preference must not override somebody’s essential access or usability requirement.
- **Next Action**: Outline the next steps, such as scheduling a test drive or consulting an expert.

This structured approach ensures all voices are heard and decisions are based on comprehensive information.

## Stop Searching or Postpone

Deciding whether to stop searching or postpone the purchase hinges on resolving all essential criteria and offer terms. If the group reaches a point where all critical requirements are met and the terms are satisfactory, it may be time to finalize the decision. However, if critical evidence is missing, postponement is a valid option.

For example, if a used car's maintenance history is incomplete or a new car's delivery date is uncertain, it may be prudent to delay the decision. Document the reasons for postponement and set a timeline for revisiting the search. This ensures the decision is based on complete information rather than fatigue or impatience.

## Questions That Come Up in Shared Decisions

1. **What if we have different favourites?** Return to the same agreed brief, rather than writing a new brief to favour each car. Separate unresolved facts from taste. If both pass the essentials, explain what you prefer and what trade-off you would accept; if one still has an essential unknown, resolve it before arguing over a winner.

2. **What if one person does all the research?** Agree on the brief together, then let the researcher collect evidence against it. Other regular users should still test the practical requirements that affect them. A detailed spreadsheet cannot establish whether a passenger finds access comfortable.

3. **What if an essential feature is unknown?** Mark it pending, assign a specific question or test, and record who will follow up. Do not replace the requirement with a different feature because the evidence is inconvenient to obtain. If nobody can establish the answer, keep that uncertainty visible in the decision.

4. **What if nobody likes the compromise?** Review whether the brief contains conflicting essentials or whether the disagreement concerns preferences. Broaden the search or postpone if necessary. A shared purchase does not require declaring a car suitable when an important user’s needs remain unmet. Record what would need to change before restarting.
